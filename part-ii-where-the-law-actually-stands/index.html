Part II

Where the Law Actually Stands

This chapter matters more than most readers expect, because a compound's legal status tells you a great deal about how thoroughly it has been examined — and determines whether what you can buy has been made under any meaningful quality oversight.

The three categories that matter

FDA-approved drugs. The manufacturer ran clinical trials, submitted the data, and the FDA concluded the benefits outweigh the risks for a specific population and use. The product is made under pharmaceutical manufacturing standards, the label is legally binding, and side effects are tracked after launch. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, tesamorelin, teriparatide, abaloparatide, bremelanotide and afamelanotide are in this group.

Compounded preparations. Compounding pharmacies prepare customised medications for individual patients under Sections 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and do not undergo premarket review for safety or efficacy. For a pharmacy to compound with a given bulk substance under 503A, that substance must have a USP or National Formulary monograph, be a component of an approved drug, or appear on the FDA's 503A Bulks List.

"Research use only" products. Sold online, labelled not for human consumption, and outside the prescription drug supply chain entirely. There is no regulatory review of identity, purity, sterility, or dose. The "research use only" label is the legal mechanism that allows the sale; it is not a description of who buys the product.

What changed in 2023, and again in 2026

This area has moved considerably, and much of what you'll read online is out of date.

In September 2023, the FDA placed a large group of popular peptides into Category 2 of the interim 503A Bulks List — the designation for substances that raise significant safety concerns [20]. The substances added included AOD-9604, BPC-157, LL-37, CJC-1295, dihexa acetate, emideltide (DSIP), epitalon, injectable GHK-Cu, ipamorelin acetate, kisspeptin-10, KPV, Melanotan II, PEG-MGF, MOTS-c, selank, semax, thymosin alpha-1 and a thymosin beta-4 fragment [20]. The practical effect was that licensed compounding pharmacies could no longer prepare them, while the unregulated online market was untouched.

Then the picture shifted. On April 15, 2026, the FDA removed BPC-157, along with eleven other peptides, from Category 2 entirely [21]. That sounds like good news, and it is widely misread as such. But the FDA did not move them to Category 1 — the list of substances permitted for compounding — and neither BPC-157 nor TB-500 is FDA-approved or has a recognised USP/NF monograph, leaving them in a regulatory gray zone where they are neither explicitly prohibited nor authorised [21].

Most recently, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met on July 23–24, 2026 to consider seven peptides for the 503A Bulks List. The committee voted in favour of BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, epitalon and semax, while recommending against emideltide [22]. Two details make this vote less decisive than the headlines suggested. FDA staff had recommended against inclusion of all the peptides before the committee reached a different conclusion, and the votes did not make these peptides lawful for compounding — the PCAC serves an advisory role, and the FDA retains authority to decide what is placed on the list [22]. All six favourable votes passed by narrow margins [23].

What this means for you

Three things, and they hold regardless of how the rulemaking eventually lands.

An advisory vote is not an approval. None of these compounds became an FDA-approved medicine in 2026. Inclusion on a compounding list — if it happens — would mean a pharmacy may legally prepare the substance on a prescription. It would not mean the compound has been shown to work.

Regulatory movement does not create evidence. The reason the FDA's own reviewers recommended against these substances is that the underlying human studies remain short in duration, small in sample size, and insufficient to establish safety or efficacy for the proposed indications [23]. A committee vote did not change that.

Nothing here legitimises the online research-chemical market. Whatever happens with compounding rules applies to licensed pharmacies dispensing against a prescription. It has no bearing on products sold in vials from unverified suppliers.

Because this area is actively moving, treat any regulatory statement in this guide — including these — as accurate at the time of writing and worth re-checking before you act on it.

A note on sport

Several compounds in this guide are prohibited in competitive sport. WADA's S0 category covers substances with no approval from any governmental health authority for human therapeutic use, which sweeps in most research peptides by definition. Growth-hormone-releasing peptides and secretagogues are prohibited under S2. If you compete in a tested sport, assume a research peptide is banned unless you have confirmed otherwise with your anti-doping authority.
